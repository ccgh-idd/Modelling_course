By the end of this lecture, you will understand:

  • The drivers of indirect protection from vaccination

Why Model Vaccination?

Vaccination decisions require understanding:

  • Direct effects: How does vaccination protect vaccinated individuals?
  • Indirect effects: How does vaccination of some protect others?
  • Long-term dynamics: How does waning immunity affect control?
  • Program design: Which age groups and coverage levels achieve control goals?
Key insight: The population impact of vaccination depends critically on how we model vaccine protection, not just that there is protection.

All-or-Nothing vs. Leaky Vaccine Protection

All-or-Nothing

A fraction of vaccinated individuals are fully protected (immune), others get no benefit.

Example: Measles vaccine: ~95% fully immune, ~5% not immune.

Leaky Protection

All vaccinated individuals have reduced susceptibility, but are not fully immune.

Example: Influenza vaccine: all vaccinated individuals have reduced infection risk.

Mathematical implication: These model differently in transmission equations and produce different predictions for herd immunity thresholds.

Modeling All-or-Nothing Protection

In an all-or-nothing model:

  • Vaccinated population splits into Protected (P) and Susceptible (S) compartments
  • Only susceptible vaccinated individuals contribute to transmission and infection
Example compartments:

S → V_p (protected) + V_s (susceptible, unprotected)

Herd immunity threshold: lower coverage needed due to complete protection in responders

Interpretation: This model is appropriate when vaccine efficacy is high and uniform within responders.

Modeling Leaky Protection

In a leaky model:

  • All vaccinated individuals remain in the Susceptible compartment
  • But their susceptibility is reduced by a factor ε (efficacy, 0-1)
  • Force of infection on vaccinated: FOI × (1 - ε)
Force of Infection calculation:

For unvaccinated: λ = β × I / N

For vaccinated: λ_v = β × (1 - ε) × I / N

Interpretation: This model is appropriate when vaccine protection is partial and affects transmission risk equally across all vaccinated individuals.

Population-Level Differences

All-or-Nothing at 60% coverage with 90% efficacy

54% population protected + 40% susceptible = 94% immune

Leaky at 60% coverage with 90% efficacy

40% fully susceptible + 60% with 90% reduced risk

Key difference: Leaky models show lower herd immunity thresholds and greater population-level protection from vaccination coverage.

The choice between these models depends on biological understanding of your vaccine.

Infection vs. Disease Protection

Vaccines can protect at different steps in the infection pathway:

Protection Against Infection

VE_i: Reduces susceptibility to acquiring infection

Example: Rotavirus vaccine

Protection Against Disease

VE_d: Reduces probability of disease given infection occurs

Example: Varicella vaccine

Modeling implication: With disease-only protection, vaccinated individuals can still be infected and transmit, creating different indirect effects.

Waning Vaccine Immunity

Vaccine protection often decreases over time:

  • Boosters required: Some vaccines need repeated doses to maintain protection
  • Duration of immunity: Varies from months to decades depending on vaccine and disease
  • Population structure: Creates cohorts with different immunity levels by vaccination date
Example: Pertussis vaccine immunity wanes over 5-10 years, requiring boosters in adolescence and adulthood.

Modeling approach: Track vaccinated cohorts by time since vaccination, transition to waned compartments over time.

Modeling Waning Mathematically

  • Approach 1 (simple): Vaccinated compartment with fixed duration, then return to susceptible
  • Approach 2 (realistic): Graduated waning - efficacy decreases gradually over time
  • Approach 3 (complex): Specify full immunity kinetics from immunological data
Trade-off: More realism in waning requires more data and parameters to estimate. Choose appropriate complexity for your question.

Impact: Waning affects long-term disease control and optimal booster schedules.

Indirect Effects: Herd Immunity

Indirect protection: Unvaccinated individuals are protected because others are vaccinated, reducing disease circulation.

  • Unvaccinated people face lower force of infection when prevalence is low
  • Effect is strongest when vaccination coverage is high
  • Herd immunity threshold depends on R₀ and vaccine efficacy
Example: Measles with R₀ ≈ 15 requires ~95% population immunity. With 90% vaccine efficacy, need ~97% vaccination coverage.

Key for public health: Even unvaccinated people benefit when coverage is sufficiently high.

Other Important Indirect Effects

  • Age-shifting: Vaccination can delay infection to older ages, potentially increasing severity
  • Serotype replacement: Vaccination against one strain can allow others to emerge
  • Immune interference: One infection can reduce susceptibility to another pathogen
  • Vaccine-strain transmission: Live vaccines can transmit to unvaccinated contacts (beneficial in some settings)
  • Behavioral changes: People may take more risk if they perceive protection
Modeling note: These effects are often context-specific and require careful consideration for policy-relevant modeling.

Putting It Together: A Simple SVIR Model

Model structure:

  • S (Susceptible) → V (Vaccinated) at rate determined by coverage
  • V can transition to I at reduced rate (vaccine efficacy)
  • I → R (Recovered) at disease-specific rate
Key equations:

dS/dt = -β × S × I / N

dV/dt = vaccination_rate - β × (1 - VE) × V × I / N

dI/dt = β × (S + (1-VE) × V) × I / N - γ × I

Interpreting Results from Vaccine Models

When analyzing vaccine model outputs, consider:

  • Peak infections: Does vaccination reduce the height of epidemic peaks?
  • Attack rates: What proportion of population gets infected, vaccinated vs. unvaccinated?
  • Critical coverage: What vaccination coverage eliminates endemic transmission?
  • Indirect protection: How much benefit accrues to the unvaccinated?
  • Sensitivity: How robust are results to uncertainty in vaccine efficacy or coverage?

Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Model structure matters: All-or-nothing vs. leaky protection produces different predictions
  • Distinguish mechanisms: Know whether vaccine protects infection, disease, or transmission
  • Account for dynamics: Waning immunity and age structure shape long-term impact
  • Quantify indirect effects: Herd immunity thresholds and population-level benefits require population-level modeling
  • Communicate assumptions: Policy-makers need to understand your modeling choices and their implications
