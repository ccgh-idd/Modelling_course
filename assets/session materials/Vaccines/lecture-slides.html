Vaccine Models in Mathematical Epidemiology

Conceptualizing vaccine protection and its population-level impacts

Stefan Flasche

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, you will understand:

  • The differences between all-or-nothing and leaky vaccine protection
  • How to distinguish vaccine protection against infection vs. disease
  • Mechanisms and modeling of waning vaccine protection
  • The drivers of indirect protection from vaccination
  • Other important indirect effects of vaccination programs

Why Model Vaccination?

Vaccination decisions require understanding:

  • Direct effects: How does vaccination protect vaccinated individuals?
  • Indirect effects: How does vaccination of some protect others?
  • Long-term dynamics: How does waning immunity affect control?
  • Program design: Which age groups and coverage levels achieve control goals?
Key insight: The population impact of vaccination depends critically on how we model vaccine protection, not just that there is protection.

Infection vs. Disease Protection

A vaccine can block the acquisition of infection, or only the disease that follows it:

Only protection against infection changes transmission. If a vaccine prevents disease alone, vaccinees are infected and infectious as before — there are no indirect effects, and a static cohort calculation of averted cases is enough. No transmission model needed.

Waning Protection

Protection is rarely permanent — and the shape you assume for its decay is a modelling choice:

The shape matters, not just the average duration. Exponential waning leaks individuals back to full susceptibility at a constant rate; a plateau-then-decline holds a cohort and then loses it together. They imply different booster timing and different long-term control.

Example: protection after the fifth dose of acellular pertussis vaccine declines markedly within ~5 years — Klein et al., N Engl J Med 2012.

Indirect Effects: Herd Immunity

Indirect protection: Unvaccinated individuals are protected because others are vaccinated, reducing disease circulation.

  • Unvaccinated people face lower force of infection when prevalence is low
  • Effect is strongest when vaccination coverage is high
  • Herd immunity threshold depends on R₀ and vaccine efficacy
Example: Measles with R₀ ≈ 15 requires ~95% population immunity. With 90% vaccine efficacy, need ~97% vaccination coverage.

Key for public health: Even unvaccinated people benefit when coverage is sufficiently high.

Other Important Indirect Effects

Beyond herd immunity, vaccination reshapes the ecology it acts on:

  • Age-shifting: infection is delayed to ages where disease is worse
  • Serotype replacement: the vacated niche is refilled by other strains
  • Pathogen interactions: preventing one infection changes the burden of others
  • Vaccine-strain transmission: live vaccines spread — and can revert
  • Behavioural response: perceived safety may change contact patterns
All five are indirect: none appears in a model of vaccinated individuals alone. Each needs the transmission system, and each has changed a real policy.

Age-Shifting: Rubella and CRS

Vaccination lowers the force of infection, so those still susceptible meet the virus later in life — and for rubella, later can mean pregnancy.

Partial coverage can be worse than none. Greece reached moderate, patchy MMR uptake through the 1980s and then recorded its largest congenital rubella epidemic in 1993 — the outcome transmission models had predicted a decade earlier.

Anderson & May, J Hyg 1983 · Panagiotopoulos et al., BMJ 1999 (Greece)

Serotype Replacement: Pneumococcus

Conjugate vaccines target a subset of serotypes — but the niche they occupied does not stay empty.

Carriage stays roughly constant; only its composition changes. Vaccine types are cleared from the nasopharynx and non-vaccine types expand into the space. The net benefit is therefore set by how much less invasive the replacing serotypes are — not by the vaccine-type decline alone.

Weinberger, Malley & Lipsitch, Lancet 2011 · Flasche et al., PLoS Medicine 2011 (England & Wales)

Pathogen Interactions: Measles Immune Amnesia

Measles infection destroys existing immune memory, so preventing it also prevents deaths from entirely different pathogens.

The effect is large enough to see in mortality data. Measles incidence predicts non-measles childhood infectious mortality for two to three years afterwards, which explains a substantial share of the mortality decline that followed measles vaccination.

Mina et al., Science 2015 (mortality) · Mina et al., Science 2019 (antibody repertoire)

Vaccine-Strain Transmission: Oral Polio Vaccine

A live vaccine is itself transmissible — which cuts both ways.

Both branches are real. OPV's transmissibility extended immunity well beyond those actually vaccinated — a large part of its success. But where coverage is low the vaccine virus circulates long enough to regain virulence, and circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus has in recent years caused more paralytic polio worldwide than wild virus.

Fine & Carneiro, American Journal of Epidemiology 1999 · GPEI surveillance

Behavioural Response: Risk Compensation

A vaccine changes perceived risk, and perceived risk can change behaviour — a loop your model contains only if you put it there.

Plausible, but rarely observed. The best-studied case is HPV vaccination, where cohort studies found no increase in sexual risk behaviour or in sexually transmitted infections among vaccinated adolescents. Treat risk compensation as a hypothesis for sensitivity analysis, not a default assumption.

Smith et al., CMAJ 2015 (Ontario cohort) · Jena et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2015

Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Model structure matters: All-or-nothing vs. leaky protection produces different predictions
  • Distinguish mechanisms: Know whether vaccine protects infection, disease, or transmission
  • Account for dynamics: Waning immunity and age structure shape long-term impact
  • Quantify indirect effects: Herd immunity thresholds and population-level benefits require population-level modeling
  • Communicate assumptions: Policy-makers need to understand your modeling choices and their implications
